How does this work?

Each galaxy is a heavy ball of mass – its stars, gas and invisible dark matter – and its stars are shown as dots (each dot stands for millions of real stars). The two galaxies pull on each other with gravity. Their stars feel both pulls, which flings out long tails of stars. As the galaxies pass through each other, gravity drags on them, slows them down, and they fall back together until they merge into one big, round (elliptical) galaxy.

Star crashes are worked out separately with real numbers: how many stars each galaxy has, how tightly they are packed, the size of a star like the Sun and how fast the stars pass each other (gravity helps by bending their paths a little). Crashes are counted while the galaxies collide, until they have merged into one. Each bright flash stands for one or more crashes. It is an estimate from a simple model, so treat the number as "about": scientists say that when the Milky Way and Andromeda meet, almost no stars will hit each other.

The Milky Way and Andromeda start at their real distance today (2.5 million light years) and real speed (about 110 km/s towards each other). Every other pair starts closer, so you do not wait so long. Time is measured from the start.
